In October 2026 I ran a series of computer searches with an AI assistant on open problems whose best known answers are kept in public tables. They improved entries in those tables and found the exact value for four of them. They also found a smaller counterexample to a 2025 conjecture and two small errors in another 2025 paper. Every result comes with a file anyone can check.

Meeting schedules (covering designs)

The puzzle

A group of people meets in smaller groups of a fixed size, and every pair of people must sit together in at least one meeting; harder versions ask the same of every three or four people. The fewest meetings that can manage this is called a covering number. For most group sizes nobody knows it, so a public table records the best schedules found so far.

What I found

With 68 people meeting 6 at a time, the best known schedule needed 160 meetings. I found one with 159, and no schedule can use fewer, so this case is now solved. The same happened with 63 people in groups of 11 (42 meetings down to 41), 76 people in groups of 9 (86 down to 85) and 86 people in groups of 7 (186 down to 185). In all, 27 cases got shorter schedules than the table listed.

How long it stood

The four solved cases had stood since 2008 and 2009, and the other 23 records dated from 2009 to March 2026. The table itself has been kept for about 30 years, first as the La Jolla Covering Repository and now at coveringrepository.com.

Why it matters

The table is a standard reference in combinatorics, the mathematics of arrangements, and designs of this kind are used to plan experiments and to build lottery systems. Most of the improvements are small, one to three meetings fewer; the four solved cases are final.

Colourings (Ramsey theory)

The puzzle

Colour the numbers 1, 2, 3 and onwards with 7 colours so that no colour contains two numbers together with their sum. The question is how far you can go before that becomes impossible; the answer is called a Schur number.

What I found

A colouring of the numbers from 1 to 1698. The best known before went to 1696. With a construction by Ageron and co-authors, the same colouring also leads to longer colourings than known before for 12 colours, and for 10 colours in a weaker version of the puzzle where a number may share a colour with its double.

How long it stood

The 1696 had stood since 2021, when F. Rowley improved on the 1680 found in 2000. My colouring starts from Rowley's.

Why it matters

Schur numbers go back to 1916 and are among the oldest questions in Ramsey theory, the mathematics of patterns that cannot be avoided. The colouring also raises a bound on a related Ramsey number from 1698 to 1700. The step is small: two numbers further.

  • doi:10.5281/zenodo.23670664
Details

Schur number S(7) ≥ 1698, against 1696 (F. Rowley, arXiv:2107.03560, 2021; OEIS A045652), which improved 1680 (Fredricksen and Sweet, 2000). It gives R₇(3) ≥ 1700, where Radziszowski's survey DS1, revision 18, lists 1698. The archive's script checks every pair. With the template inequalities S(n+5) ≥ 380 S(n) + 148 and WS(n+3) ≥ 42 S(n) + 24 of Ageron, Casteras, Pellerin, Portella, Rimmel and Tomasik (arXiv:2112.03175), the 1698 gives S(12) ≥ 645388 and WS(10) ≥ 71340 (previously 644628 and 71256); version 2 of the record adds both colourings and a script that checks them exactly.

Codes (coding theory)

The puzzle

Choose as few strings of 20 zeros and ones as possible so that every such string differs from one of the chosen ones in at most 3 positions. A set like this is called a covering code, and the smallest possible size is unknown.

What I found

A set of 1905 strings that does the job. The published tables listed 2048 as the best known.

How long it stood

The tables had listed 2048 since at least 1999, and the main one has not changed since 2009.

Why it matters

Covering codes are a classic topic in coding theory, the mathematics behind sending and storing data reliably, and tables like these are how the field keeps score. The gap to the best known lower bound, 892, stays wide.

  • doi:10.5281/zenodo.23670398
Details

Binary covering code K(20,3) ≤ 1905, against 2048 in Kéri's table (unchanged since 2009, lower bound 892) and in Litsyn's table (last modified 1999), where 2048 comes from a linear code. The archive's script checks all 2^20 words exactly; a second program, written separately by another Claude session, agrees.

Networks (graph theory)

A smaller counterexample to a 2025 conjecture

The puzzle

In 2025 four mathematicians published a conjecture in the Electronic Journal of Combinatorics: a certain inequality about networks should hold for every network. They proved it in some cases and checked every network with up to 9 nodes by computer.

What I found

A network with 14 nodes, built from the Fano plane, the smallest of the finite geometries called projective planes, where the inequality fails, and whole infinite families of networks where it fails as well. So the conjecture is false.

How long it stood

The conjecture was published in 2025. W. Jiang disproved it in September 2026 with a network of 16 nodes.

Why it matters

With 14 nodes it is the smallest counterexample I know of: Jiang's smallest has 16, and he leaves open whether a smaller one exists. The infinite families, built from projective planes and related designs, are not part of his construction. The note is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed yet.

  • doi:10.5281/zenodo.23669060
  • W. Jiang, doi:10.5281/zenodo.22238691
Details

Conjecture 20 of Chen, Guo, Li and Wang (Electron. J. Combin. 32(4) (2025) #P4.37), on the k-th Laplacian eigenvalue of a graph and its complement, was first disproved by W. Jiang (Zenodo, doi:10.5281/zenodo.22238691) with a 16-vertex counterexample at k = 7. It also fails for the split graph of the Fano plane with n = 14 and k = 6, and for the split graphs of infinitely many symmetric designs, none of them in Jiang's construction. The record holds exact proofs and a verification script.

Larger symmetric networks

The puzzle

Connect as many computers as possible when each may have a fixed number of cables, so that any message reaches any other computer in at most two hops. Public tables list the largest such networks known, with a separate table for networks that have a strong kind of symmetry.

What I found

For five cable counts I found symmetric networks larger than that table lists, for example 468 computers with 27 cables each, where the table had 392.

How long it stood

The table of symmetric networks was last updated in 2022.

Why it matters

The question comes from designing computer and communication networks. For all five cable counts, larger networks without this symmetry are already known, so these are records for the symmetric kind only.

  • doi:10.5281/zenodo.23668463
Details

Five Cayley graphs of diameter 2 whose orders exceed the Cayley-graph table of the degree-diameter problem (combinatoricswiki). Larger graphs without the Cayley property are known for all five degrees: the general-graph table lists 307, 338 and 381 for degrees 18 to 20, and those 381 at degree 20 and Brown's 651 at degree 26 already exceed 320 at degree 22 and 468 at degree 27.

Corrections to a second 2025 paper

The puzzle

A second 2025 paper, in the journal Filomat, proposed two related conjectures about networks, again an inequality that should hold for every network.

What I found

As printed, both conjectures are slightly wrong. One misses an exception, the ring of five nodes. The other fails for networks made of separate pieces, such as a ring of five nodes next to a single node. The proof of a lemma that reduces the question to networks in one piece also has a gap. The note gives these small counterexamples and a corrected version of the lemma.

How long it stood

The paper was published in 2025. The main question, for networks in one piece, is still open.

Why it matters

It is a small correction to the record: it fixes how the conjectures are stated and leaves the main question open. The note is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed yet.

  • doi:10.5281/zenodo.23669770
Details

W.-J. Li and J.-M. Guo, Filomat 39:13 (2025) 4551-4564: Conjecture 1.2 fails for the 5-cycle, an exception lost when it is derived from Conjecture 1.1, and Conjecture 1.1 fails for disconnected graphs such as C5 + K1, because the proof of Lemma 2.1 assumes that every component satisfies the inequality. The record holds exact computations and a corrected Lemma 2.1.

Geometry

Packing coins in a triangle

The puzzle

Fit a given number of identical coins, without overlapping, into a right-angled triangle with two equal sides, and make the coins as large as possible.

What I found

For two numbers of coins, 255 and 284, I found arrangements with larger coins than any public record I could find. The coins grow by at most 0.07 percent.

How long it stood

For both, the record had been set by Moki&Julio in September 2026.

Why it matters

Packomania's tables are where packing records are compared, and questions like this one are close relatives of practical ones, such as cutting material with little waste.

  • doi:10.5281/zenodo.23668593
Details

Packings of equal circles in the isosceles right triangle with legs 1, each radius certified in exact decimal arithmetic. For N = 255 and 284 the radii beat Packomania's table and Moki&Julio's values. The record also holds packings for N = 164, 182 and 193; larger radii for those had already been published by Lai, Hao, Yue and Zhou (Computers & Operations Research 181, 2025), a paper I found only after publishing.

Terence Tao's list of open problems

The puzzle

Terence Tao, a mathematician who won the Fields Medal in 2006, keeps a public list of mathematical constants whose exact values are unknown. For each one it records the best bounds proved so far, and researchers, some of them working with AI systems, keep improving them.

What I found

A better upper bound for one of these constants, 1d, built on a certificate that another contributor, echul-seal, posted earlier the same day. It comes with a file anyone can check exactly. A second bound, for 1c, was withdrawn: Shenai Chen and EnHao Gu had already published a better one on arXiv on 8 October.

How long it stood

Constant 1d joined the list on 12 October 2026, and its bound has been improved several times since.

Why it matters

The step is small, in the fifth decimal place. The submission is awaiting review and has not been accepted yet.

Details

An exact step-function certificate for the constant 1d in Terence Tao's repository of optimization constants, refining the certificate in pull request #269 by echul-seal. The certificate for 1c stays public in the same repository so that it can still be checked; its value is below the bound of Chen and Gu (arXiv:2610.18888).
